CyberPatriot is a team event with multiple images. How you divide the work is
a real decision, and the wrong split wastes more time than any individual
technique saves.

## Divide by machine, not by task

The cleanest boundary is one person per image. It removes overlap by
construction — nobody is editing a file someone else is editing, and each
machine has exactly one person who knows its state.

Dividing by task instead ("you do accounts everywhere, I do services
everywhere") sounds efficient and works badly. Everyone needs context on
every machine, everyone reads every README, and two people end up in the same
config file.

Put your strongest person on the hardest image, not on the one they like most.

## When you have more people than images

Pair up, but give each pair a clear split *within* the machine:

- One drives — hands on the keyboard, makes every change.
- One navigates — reads the checklist aloud, keeps the change log, watches the
  score report.

This is genuinely faster than two people typing on one box, and it makes the
log something that actually gets written rather than something everyone
intends to write.

Swap roles occasionally. The navigator learns less by watching, and a team
where only one person can drive is fragile.

## One machine, one log, one owner

Whoever owns a machine owns its change log. Not a shared document across all
machines — one per machine, kept by the person making the changes.

When a score drops on that image, there is one record and one person who knows
what is in it. Split records are how a team ends up unable to reconstruct
what happened.

## What to share across the team

Some things are worth doing once and distributing:

- The **README extraction** — authorized users, admins, required services.
  Different per machine, but the *habit* of extracting is a team standard.
- The **audit script** — written once, used on every image, improved each
  round.
- **Findings that generalise.** "This image has a rogue authorized_keys" is
  worth saying out loud; the others should check theirs.

## Check in, briefly

Every hour, thirty seconds each: what have you finished, what is stuck, where
is your score. It costs almost nothing and it catches the two failures that
actually hurt — one person silently stuck on the same item for an hour, and a
whole category nobody has touched because each of you assumed the other had.

## After the round

Debrief while it is fresh. What scored, what did not, what surprised you, what
you would put in the checklist for next time. The teams that improve fastest
are the ones that treat the checklist and the audit script as things that grow
after every round rather than starting over each time.
