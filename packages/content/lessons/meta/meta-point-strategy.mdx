Every item on an image is worth points, but they are not worth the same
points, they do not cost the same time, and they do not carry the same risk.
Ordering them well is worth more than knowing any individual fix.

## Forensics first, always

Forensics questions cannot break the machine and they cannot be undone by a
mistake. They are also the only category with a hard ordering constraint:
fixing a vulnerability destroys the evidence. Delete the malicious user and
the question asking for its UID becomes unanswerable.

Zero risk plus a one-way door means they go first. Every time.

## Then the cheap, certain, reversible ones

After forensics, work in rough order of certainty:

1. **Start the package update.** It takes the longest and runs unattended.
   Kick it off and let it work while you do everything else.
2. **Account items.** Unauthorized users, unauthorized admins, blank
   passwords. Well-defined, directly scored, low risk once you have the
   README's list.
3. **Password and lockout policy.** A handful of settings, all scored, none
   of which can break a service.
4. **The obvious service removals.** Telnet and friends, once you have
   confirmed the README does not require them.

## Then the ones that need care

SSH configuration, firewall rules, and anything touching a running service.
These are scored, but each has a way to lock you out or break something. Test
before restarting — `sshd -t`, `apache2ctl configtest` — and allow before you
enable with `ufw`.

## The risks that are never worth it

**Never stop a service the README requires.** The penalty exceeds what you
would gain, and it is the single most common way teams lose points they
already had.

**Never delete an authorized account**, however suspicious the name.

**Never make a change you cannot undo** with your change log and backups.

The asymmetry is the point. A missed vulnerability costs you one item. A
broken required service costs you a penalty *and* everything downstream of
that service. When the downside is bigger than the upside, the cheap check
always wins.

## Scoring feedback is delayed

The engine polls on an interval. A change might take a minute or two to
register, which has two consequences.

Do not sit and watch the report after every change — batch your work and let
the score catch up. And when the score drops, the cause is not necessarily the
thing you did most recently. That is exactly what the change log is for.

## The last thirty minutes

Stop starting new things. Confirm required services are still running, skim
your change log for anything half-finished, and re-read the README against
what you actually did.

Late in a round your downside is larger than your upside. A speculative fix
you have never tested can cost more than it earns; verifying you have not
broken anything protects points you have already banked.
